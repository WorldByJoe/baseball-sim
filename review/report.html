American League Division Series · Yankees at Rays · Games 1 and 2

The Yankees lost two games they were favored to win

Reading every player's 2025 and 2026 numbers into the simulation, the Yankees went into both games in St. Petersburg as the stronger side: 52% behind Gerrit Cole in Game 1 and 60% behind Cam Schlittler in Game 2. They lost 1-0 and 5-2. Most of the gap came from balls in play that did not fall, four Yankee defensive miscues in Game 2, and a Freddy Peralta who threw harder than he had all year.

Where the runs went

Each plate appearance was replayed a thousand times between the real batter and the real pitcher, at the pitcher's pitch count, with what the batter had already seen of him. Each batted ball was set beside the forty most similar balls hit in 2025, matched on exit speed, launch angle and direction. The tables put what happened beside what those matchups predicted.

Game 2 · Rays 5, Yankees 2

The Yankees made about 1.8 runs less than their matchups predicted, all of it on contact. Their 24 balls in play would on average have produced 6.3 hits and produced four, an outcome about one game in nine. The Rays' contact ran cold too, eight hits from balls worth 10.8. They beat their matchups by 1.1 runs anyway, by refusing to strike out: four strikeouts in 38 trips against 8.8 expected, about a one-in-25 outcome. Their five runs came from events worth 2.9, because their hits came in two clusters and three of the runs scored on or after Yankee errors.

Game 1 · Rays 1, Yankees 0

One hit from fifteen balls in play, against 4.1 expected. That was a one-in-45 outcome, the least likely thing in the series so far. Add twelve strikeouts against nine expected and the offense fell about five runs short of its matchups. The Rays' only run was Jonathan Aranda's 107 mph home run off Cole, an outcome the model gave 2% in that plate appearance.

Players against their priors

Peralta threw 2.4 mph harder than his season

Peralta's four-seamer averaged 96.8 mph over 43 pitches against 94.3 for his 2026 season, and his changeup was a mph firmer. Replaying Game 2 with the speeds he actually showed, the Yankees' chance fell from 60% to 56% and their expected runs from 3.7 to 3.3. The model's link from velocity to strikeouts is weaker than the league's, so that is probably an understatement. No other pitcher in either game was more than a mph off his norm over a real sample.

Schlittler had the stuff; the Rays made contact anyway

Fitted to his 31% strikeout rate, Schlittler came out as the most deceptive pitcher on either staff. The Rays struck out only twice against him and four times all game, against 8.8 expected. Their hitters' contact numbers say they are a team that rarely strikes out, which the model accounts for. Four is still well below even that.

The hitters attacked early and took late

Both lineups swung at 56% of first-pitch strikes, where the model, calibrated to the league, expected 40%. With two strikes they chased pitches outside the zone 30% of the time, where the model expected 47%. That is a playoff approach the season numbers don't fully capture, read from about 170 pitches, so treat it as a pattern to watch in Game 3 rather than a settled trait.

The umpire was not the story

Against a neutral, league-average umpire, three taken pitches across both games went the other way, all called balls on likely strikes. Two of the three favored Yankee hitters.

How this was made

  • Hitters. Each hitter's body and swing come from his measurements (height, weight, bat speed, swing length, attack angle, swing tilt). His thirteen hidden traits, such as eye, timing and barrel control, were fitted so the model reproduces his own 2025-26 swing rates, whiffs, strikeouts, walks, home runs, exit speed and batted-ball mix, with uncertainty that shrinks as his sample grows. Every replay draws his traits from that uncertainty.
  • Pitchers. Each pitcher's arm slot, release point, and every pitch's speed, spin and movement come straight from Statcast. Two hidden traits, deception and command, were fitted so his strikeout and walk rates stand to the model's league as his real ones stand to the real league.
  • Batted balls. Hit chances for real batted balls use the league's 2025 balls in play rather than the model's fielders, which are being refitted.
  • Games. Pregame and series chances come from 3,000 replays of each game with the real lineups, starters and bullpens, the real park, and postseason rules.

Limits

  • The model strikes out too few batters league-wide, and its fitted hitters hit about a fifth fewer home runs than they really do. Strikeouts and home runs look a little more surprising here than they should.
  • The model makes fewer errors than the league, so the two Game 2 errors read as rarer than they were.
  • Defensive substitutions are not followed, and managers in the replays pull starters by fatigue, not by playoff instinct.
  • The playoff rosters date from October 4; the data cover 42 days of 2025 and the full 2026 season.
